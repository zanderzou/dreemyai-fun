Give an original character a location, a reason to speak and a choice the reader can make. Put personality and longer backstory in the character brief. The greeting has a smaller job: make the first reply easy to write.

Dreemy's public FAQ describes personality, backstory and appearance as character details and distinguishes private originals from public characters. The same opening can work in either setting. If you plan for public use, write it for a stranger who has never seen your private notes.

## Set one problem the character can see

Sample greeting for an adult station clerk: “The last train is delayed. A light is still on in the ticket office. We can wait by the stairs or ask the clerk for an update. Which would you prefer?”

The delay gives the characters a reason to talk. Waiting preserves their place in the queue. Asking gives the scene a chance to move. The reader can also refuse both options without breaking the setup. Contrast that with a sentence that says the reader already slipped into the office. It closes the choice before the conversation begins.

![A character greeting places the characters, offers two actions and waits for the reader's reply.](/assets/editorial-dreemy-original-character-first-message.png)

## Read it from three possible starts

Try writing three next lines on paper: “Let's wait,” “Ask for an update,” and “I'll leave.” If the character opening only makes sense after one of those, remove the assumed decision. Give the character one fact that stays true regardless of which path the reader picks.

Check the public-facing name and greeting for real emails, workplace details and identifying references taken from private notes. Dreemy's privacy policy warns against putting identifying information in public prompts or bots. A fictional station can provide enough texture without using the name of a real colleague.

Save the greeting on its own. You can then revise the opening after a read-through without changing all the character's background at once.
